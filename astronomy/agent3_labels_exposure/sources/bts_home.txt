 Sample Explorer 
 The Zwicky Transient Facility ( ZTF ) Bright Transient Survey is by far the largest spectroscopic supernova survey ever conducted. 
Since June of 2018 we have been surveying the entire nighttime Northern sky every 2-3 nights in two filters 
and acquiring spectra of (almost) every time-varying, nonmoving object brighter than 18.5 mag that is not a Galactic source or an AGN, providing a complete and unbiased view of the dynamic optical sky. 
Transients down to 19.0 mag are also targeted when possible (given available spectroscopic resources). Between 2017 and 2024 we catalogued over 10000 supernovae and hundreds of "exotic" transients: tidal disruption events, superluminous supernovae, and the like.
 While the prime survey is completed, the BTS continues in modified form into 2025: we
are continuing to survey the ZTF public fields and classify all transients brighter than 18.0 mag, and all transients coincident with known galaxies within 150 Mpc are targeted for observations down to the survey limit of about 20 mag ( CATS150 ).
 So far, our efforts have detected 11580 spectroscopically-confirmed transients brighter than 19 mag (11431 supernovae and 149 transients of other types). The large majority of these classifications were provided by our programs.
 Alert data (including preliminary photometry) is released in real time via ZTF Alert Brokers ( 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ) and spectra and classifications are announced nightly via TNS .
 Cumulative properties of the sample are compiled here and in our papers . Some properties of the current sample can be explored in detail using the BTS Sample Explorer .

 Survey Design
 Transients are provided by the Northern Sky Survey, a 3-night-cadence g+r survey of the whole extragalactic sky that is ZTF's primary public data stream. A software filter scans the ~1 million nightly "alerts" from this stream and identifies real, non-stellar, non-AGN sources above our magnitude cut, generating ~100 supernova candidates per night. A team of human scanners (mostly students, based around the world) examines the output to remove any remaining false positives and assigns the remaining targets for spectroscopy at robotic telescopes at Palomar (P60/SEDM) or La Palma.
 Spectrum requests are inserted into a queue and typically observed within a few days, and reduced automatically. A combination of classification software and expert inspection are used to determine the redshifts and classifications. If the type is ambiguous, the object is re-inserted into the queue for observations. Classical runs at larger telescopes bolster the effort. Numerous automatic checkers and cross-checkers operate as a fail-safe to ensure nothing fades away before it is classified.
 Our goal is to classify every target that is plausibly a supernova and reaches a recorded peak magnitude brighter than 18.5 mag. Other transients (fainter transients, and SNe whose peak was not captured) are classified as well when sufficient spectroscopic resources are available. Statistics on our success rate are provided in the below links.
 All new SN classifications are sent to TNS within 24 hours and the associated spectra are also provided (with rare exceptions, generally for classifications based on proprietary data from other ZTF partners not associated with our effort).  Completeness
 Our goal is to be close to 100% spectroscopically complete for all genuine extragalactic transients brighter 
than 18.5 magnitude
that are detected in more than one imaging epoch in the survey footprint, for which the epoch of maximum light
is clearly observed, and which do not set within about a month after maximum light. (Transients that do not
meet these cuts are also observed when possible.) Our actual completeness for events meeting all these criteria is about 95%. 
(The remaining 5% are mostly concentrated in periods of bad weather that affected our spectroscopic follow-up programs.)

Sky-area coverage during summer 2018 was much reduced due to reference-building. P48 was closed for maintenance for almost all of October 2018. 
Most of March 2020 was lost due to weather (3 consecutive weeks of rain/cloud) and few events met the sample criteria. Extensive camera downtime between Dec 2021 and Apr 2022
meant that no events had sufficient coverage to pass the sample criteria in this period.
The most recent 1-2 months are generally undercounts, since classifications of declining objects are still being performed.
These statistics include some events for which classifications have not yet been publicly released. The completeness estimates are lower limits at the bright end, as most unclassified transients brighter than 18 mag are (unconfirmed) CV's and not extragalactic.
